# Mercy for the Enemy: A City Repents

The word of the Lord comes to Jonah a second time. The same command. The same mission. Go to Nineveh.

This time, Jonah goes.

## Second chances

> "Then the word of the Lord came to Jonah the second time, saying, 'Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it the message that I tell you.'"

God does not abandon His purposes. He does not find a new prophet. He recommissions the one who failed.

This is grace: God uses broken instruments. He gives second chances to runaways.

**Failure is not final with God.**

Jonah arose and went. The rebellion is over, at least outwardly. He obeys the command, though his heart remains conflicted.

## The greatness of Nineveh

> "Now Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, three days' journey in breadth."

Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, Israel's most brutal enemy. A city known for violence, cruelty, and conquest. To Israel, Nineveh represented everything threatening and evil.

And God sends His prophet there with a message.

Jonah begins to walk through the city, proclaiming: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!"

**The message is judgment. But implicit in the warning is opportunity.**

## Unexpected repentance

> "And the people of Nineveh believed God."

This is astonishing. The violent pagans believe the Hebrew prophet. They do not argue. They do not resist. They believe.

They call a fast. They put on sackcloth, from the greatest to the least. The king himself rises from his throne, removes his robe, covers himself with sackcloth, and sits in ashes.

The king issues a decree: no person or animal shall eat or drink. All shall cry out mightily to God. All shall turn from evil and violence.

> "Who knows? God may turn and relent and turn from his fierce anger, so that we may not perish."

**Nineveh repents more thoroughly than Israel ever did.**

## God relents

> "When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them, and he did not do it."

God sees their repentance. He responds with mercy. The threatened destruction does not come.

This is the heart of God: He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. He desires that they turn and live. When they turn, He relents.

**God's warnings are invitations. Judgment delayed is mercy extended.**

## The scandal of grace

Here is the tension of the book: God shows mercy to Israel's enemies.

The Assyrians were brutal oppressors. They would eventually destroy the Northern Kingdom. They represented everything Israel feared and hated.

And God spares them.

This is the scandal that Jonah cannot accept. God's mercy is not tribal. It extends to those outside the covenant. It reaches the enemies of God's people.

**Grace that makes sense is not grace. Grace for enemies is the only kind there is.**

## Final reflection

Jonah 3 records one of the greatest revivals in Scripture. An entire city, from king to cattle, repents at the preaching of a reluctant prophet.

And God responds with compassion.

This chapter forces us to ask: Do we want God's mercy for our enemies? Are we willing to be instruments of grace to those we despise?

The book of Jonah is not ultimately about Jonah. It is about the God whose compassion extends to all who repent.

## Closing prayer

Father, Your mercy is wider than our prejudice. You call enemies to repentance and spare those who turn to You. Expand our hearts to love as You love, to proclaim grace even to those we would rather see judged. In Jesus' name, amen.
